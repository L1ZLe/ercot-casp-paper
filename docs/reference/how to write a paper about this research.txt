The paper, in detail
1. What the paper is about (the research question)
Your paper adapts a specific architecture — Yu et al. 2026 arXiv:2605.09061, "A Market-Rule-Informed Neural Network for Efficient Imbalance Electricity Price Forecasting" (q-fin.CP, May 2026) — from European imbalance-price markets to the US nodal ERCOT day-ahead market, and applies it to a concrete, tradable target: day-ahead PTP (point-to-point) LMP spreads.

The core physics you exploit (the "market rule"): An ERCOT day-ahead locational marginal price at a settlement point decomposes (in simplified form) into:


LMP = λ (energy) + congestion + loss
A transmission spread between two settlement points A and B is:


spread_AB = (congestion_A − congestion_B) + (loss_A − loss_B)
The energy term λ cancels, so a spread is essentially a pure congestion differential. Congestion is driven by binding transmission constraints, each of which has a shadow price (μ_k) (the marginal cost, in $/MWh, of relieving 1 MW on that constraint).

Rewriting the standard LMP congestion formula:


congestion_spread = Σ_k ΔSF_k · μ_k
where ΔSF_k is the difference in shift factors (how 1 MW injected at a node flows through constraint k) between A and B, and μ_k is the constraint shadow price.

The novel architecture (learned-ΔSF constraint attention). Instead of treating congestion as an opaque input, your model makes the structure explicit: it learns the ΔSF directly via attention over the real per-hour binding-constraint shadow prices. The forward pass is literally:


mu_k = latent_mu_linear(slot_encoder(slot_features))          # [B, K]  ← the real shadow-price slots
Delta_SF = softmax( query(source/sink embedding) · keys ), dim=-1   # [B, K]  ← learned shift factors
spread = (Delta_SF * mu_k).sum(dim=-1)                        # energy term canceled
This makes the congestion driver interpretable by construction (attention weights ≈ which constraints matter), and it directly encodes the market settlement rule the way Yu et al. encoded European imbalance rules.

Calibration = "do my stated probabilities match reality?" Concretely: you predict a 90% interval. Calibration means the true value actually falls inside it ~90% of the time. (Your success_rate is this for the 90% band.)

The gold-standard tools to measure it (you're currently using the blunt success_rate):

PIT (Probability Integral Transform). For each forecast, take the observed value's position in your forecast CDF → a number in [0,1]. A perfectly calibrated model produces PIT values that are uniformly distributed. Deviations are diagnosable:
U-shaped PIT = intervals too narrow (overconfidence).
∩-shaped PIT = intervals too wide (underconfidence).
Left/right skew = bias/bad tails. This single plot tells you the exact failure mode and is far more rigorous than one number.
Coverage drill-down per quantile: empirical coverage at 0.10, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 0.90 — do they match nominal at every depth, not just averaged?
Winkler/interval score: combines calibration AND sharpness (a calibrated-but-ultra-wide forecast scores badly, which is correct).
CRPS: the proper scoring rule for a full distribution — a single number that rewards calibration + sharpness.
Why your model should win on calibration (mechanism, not luck): your hierarchical non-crossing quantile head forces q0.10 < q0.25 < … < q0.90 by construction and your LA-CASF penalty keeps the band well-spaced. LQR fits each quantile as an independent straight line → they can cross and mis-locate, especially in the tails. Deep nets may be well-calibrated on average but not in the tails. So the expected where-you-win is: tail quantiles (0.10, 0.90) and interval coverage — which is exactly "calibration under extreme/regime conditions."
